Narrative Research Competition

Analogy Narratives

Help us build a dataset that tests the limits of AI reasoning — by writing stories only humans can truly understand.

Why we're interested

  • Identifying analogies is a key part of human reasoning.
  • Large language models such as Claude and ChatGPT have gotten very good at complex tasks — including complex reasoning. We want to find where they still fall short.
  • We are building a dataset of challenging analogical narratives to test models' abilities, and to identify the weak spots where even the best models struggle.

Why we need you

  • There is a lack of high-quality, annotated analogical narrative datasets.
  • Creating a clever analogy that stumps a model is mentally demanding — so we made it a competition.

What is an analogy?

An analogy is a type of similarity between two stories where the relational structure of one story maps onto another — even if the individual elements look nothing alike on the surface.

For example, comparing the solar system to an atom is an analogy: a planet orbiting the sun maps onto an electron orbiting a nucleus. The objects are completely different, but the relations between them are the same.

Comparing a basketball to an orange because they share the same colour and shape is not an analogy — that is surface-level similarity, not relational similarity. The key question is always: do the relationships between things in one story mirror the relationships in the other?

What you'll submit

Three narratives, each at least 25 words:

Narrative 1 — Source
Your primary story. The main entities and their relations will map onto those in Narrative 2.
Narrative 2 — Target
A story in a different setting, with different characters — but with the same relational structure as Narrative 1.
Narrative 3 — Foil
Semantically similar to Narrative 1 (same topic, overlapping vocabulary), but not an analogy to it. It should look like it belongs, but the structural mapping with Narrative 1 breaks down.

You'll also provide the entity mappings between Narratives 1 and 2 — core entities should be mapped; superfluous noise entities don't need to be.

⚠ Do not use LLMs to write your narratives. The goal is to test AI. Models will not be stumped by text they could have written themselves — and you won't win with AI-generated entries.

⚠ Do not test your analogies on LLMs either to see how they perform. Conversations with LLMs can be used as further training data, rendering the data useless for experimentation.

A note on languages that build meaning into words. In some languages, a single word can express what English needs a whole sentence to say — for example, in Turkish, evlerinizden means "from your (plural) houses," packing house + your + plural + from into one word. If you're submitting in a language like this, you don't need to map whole words. Instead, map the meaningful pieces: the part that means "house" maps to whatever plays that role in Narrative 2, etc.

How it's judged

The best submissions will stump the best language models, while remaining relatively easy for a human to solve. Originality and conceptual distance between the two analogous domains are key.

Competition groups & prizes

Submissions are organised into three groups:

  1. English only
  2. High-resource languages — Spanish, German, Japanese, French, Arabic, Mandarin, Italian, Polish, Portuguese, Dutch, Korean, Vietnamese, Swedish, Serbo-Croatian, Russian, and Farsi
  3. Low-resource languages — any language not listed in the other groups

The best submission from each group wins a gift card worth 100 Francs / Euros / USD to Amazon or Galaxus, depending on the winner's country of residence. If neither option is available for your country, please contact the organizers to arrange an alternative.

How to write a strong submission

These are the things that are more likely to stump LLMs.

Go for distance Analogies between semantically distant domains are harder for models. A chef ↔ baker is easier than a chef ↔ astronaut, a chef ↔ penguin, or a chef ↔ bonsai tree — all of which are semantically more different.
Avoid clichés If the analogy is commonly used as a metaphor or idiom (desert = scarcity, race = competition), it's probably too obvious. The atom/solar system example provided previously would probably fall into this category.
Add noise Include a little superfluous detail in each narrative. The core entities need to map, but superfluous entities don't — extra entities that don't correspond to the other story can distract and confuse models. Additionally, mappings can be many-to-one: for example, the eight planets of the solar system listed out individually could all map to the single electron in a hydrogen atom.
Vary sentence structure Models can use identical grammar as a shortcut. If both narratives have the same syntactic shape sentence-by-sentence, that's a giveaway. Vary your phrasing.
Make the foil convincing Narrative 3 should look like an analogy on the surface — same topic as Narrative 1, similar words — but the structural mapping back to Narrative 1 should break down under scrutiny.
A better example: instead of solar system ↔ atom, consider something like a family unit ↔ atom. The electron orbiting the nucleus maps to parents fussing over their children; electromagnetism maps to love. This is harder to spot because the domains feel unrelated at first glance, and atoms are generally not used as a metaphor for familial ties and vice versa.

Example submissions

Below are short examples to illustrate the format. The first three show narrative sets that models can already solve — these are not the kind of submissions we are looking for. The final example is the kind that stumps a model, and is what we are after.

Example 1

Model can solve
A — Source
The chef was excited about the day's lunch special — an Indian inspired chicken dish. While plating the dish to serve to customers, he drizzled mint chutney over the top, and then dusted it with garam masala.
B — Target
The children danced when they heard on the news that there may be a snow day. They watched as the hail smashed against the pavement, as time for school approached. A flurry of snow followed.
C — Foil
The baker was very excited about the day's dessert special — a caramel cheesecake. He went to decorate the cake, but then realised that he forgot to order the cocoa and cinnamon he wanted to dust it with. He couldn't help but be irritated with himself for forgetting to order ingredients for a recipe he had been excited to make all week.

Example 2

Model can solve
A — Source
The young boy gazed in amazement as the parade proceeded down the avenue. A marching band, with trumpets, tubas, and drums filled the streets with music while women in colorful dresses danced.
B — Target
The bear observed hungrily as the fish proceeded upstream. Flies buzzed around in the air, as rotting fish carcasses contaminated the area with an awful stench.
C — Foil
The young boy decided to try to cross the street as the parade came to a stop in the middle of the avenue. He elbowed his way through a marching band with trumpets, tubas, and drums while women in colorful dresses shouted at him to look out.

Example 3

Model can solve
A — Source
Frances showed up to the basketball game despite her team's losing streak. To everyone's surprise, at the 10 minute mark, the forward progressed down the court. He shot the ball into the hoop and made a basket.
B — Target
The toddler slowly moved a fork full of broccoli into his mouth, to both his parents' amazement.
C — Foil
Frances was the striker on her local soccer team. She showed up to the game late, and ran straight toward the field as the goal keeper blocked the ball from entering the net.

Example 4

Stumps a model
A — Source
The beef likely was contaminated with E. Coli after sitting in the hot sun all day long.
B — Target
At first Shelly could not stand him, but eventually fell in love.
C — Foil
All day long the hot sun beat down upon the picnic. Sonja was glad she did not bring the beef dish she had originally planned, as it would have probably been contaminated with E. Coli.
